What Is Accounting and Finance Research For in the AI Era?

When writing, coding and basic synthesis become cheaper, research does not lose all value. The basis of its value changes.
Dr Yuqian Zhang, Auckland University of Technology | October 2026

Executive Summary

AI does not make accounting and finance research worthless. It makes undifferentiated research output less scarce. The main question is therefore not whether a model can produce a plausible paper. The question is what a research contribution must now contain to deserve attention, editorial capacity and public trust.

The evidence in this brief points to a shift in scarcity. Crossref-indexed research article output in 11 selected journals rose 27.9% between 2015 and 2025. In the two finance journals with usable public submission records, however, submissions did not rise continuously after large language models became widely available. Editorial acceptance remained selective, and observed median time to an editorial decision ranged from 30 to 52 days across the reported years. Citation attention could not be used to compare journals reliably, while AI guidance is now common but uneven in scope, and stated guidance specifies few research criteria.

These findings do not support a simple story of research abundance leading to research collapse. They support a more specific claim: production is not the main bottleneck. Judgement, verifiable evidence, access to difficult settings, editorial evaluation and accountable use are more likely to determine value.

  • Publication remains a threshold The evidence does not show that publication counts will disappear. It shows that a count alone is an increasingly weak account of contribution.
  • Verification becomes the constraint Cheap drafting and code generation shift effort toward checking sources, assumptions, models and claims, and toward deciding what the evidence warrants.
  • Prepare for the scarcity shift Develop a defensible problem agenda, privileged data or context, reproducible methods and the ability to make and defend decisions under uncertainty.

Introduction

Generative AI can draft a literature review, write code, propose a regression and turn results into polished prose. The larger institutional effect is less clear. If these tasks become cheap, will research become more plentiful, less valuable or differently valuable?

This brief asks what makes research trustworthy and useful, not only what makes a paper easy to produce. A paper has little value if no one can establish what it contributes. A model or dataset has little value if no one can verify how it was built. A recommendation has little value if its authors cannot explain uncertainty or accept responsibility for error. Those conditions matter more when fluent output is abundant.

The report examines six dimensions: research production, citation attention, review and gatekeeping, visible AI guidance, evidence correction and stated editorial priorities. It then connects the evidence to a practical question: what should individual researchers, doctoral programmes, journals, departments and universities do next?

Central argument. The scarce academic asset is moving from the ability to produce a paper toward the ability to choose a worthwhile problem, obtain and understand difficult evidence, verify the chain from data to claim, and make a responsible judgement that others can audit.

Background

AI is not a single technology. In this brief, the term includes generative models that write and reason with language, code-generation tools, machine learning and automated data processing. The relevant concern is not that these tools can imitate an academic article. It is that they reduce the cost of many intermediate tasks while leaving the hardest social and evidentiary tasks intact.

Experiments show clear gains in some writing tasks. Noy and Zhang found that, among 453 college-educated professionals completing mid-level writing tasks, access to ChatGPT reduced average time by 40% and raised output quality by 18% [1]. Their experiment did not study accounting or finance scholarship. It does, however, show why drafting alone is a weak professional advantage.

Evidence about research ideas is more mixed. Si, Yang and Hashimoto recruited more than 100 NLP researchers to compare expert and model-generated ideas. The model-generated ideas were judged more novel, but slightly weaker on feasibility. The authors also reported limited diversity and failures of model self-evaluation [2]. Novelty in a blinded review is not the same as a defensible contribution.

A third experiment, on short-story writing, found higher individual creativity ratings for people given AI ideas, but greater similarity across AI-assisted stories [3]. The setting is not academic research. It nevertheless illustrates a possible collective problem: a tool can improve each person's output while narrowing the range of ideas the system produces.

Direct evidence from accounting itself is now emerging. Gardner, Krupa and Mammadov (2026) analysed 3,573 accounting papers across 10 journals and 4,166 abstracts across 22 journals, and found that AI-associated vocabulary rose by about 75% after 2023, with heavier use among junior authors and lower-ranked journals [12]. A separate study of more than 15 million biomedical abstracts estimated that at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts showed signs of LLM processing [13]. These are vocabulary-based lower bounds, not verified tool use, and they say nothing about research quality. They do show that AI-assisted writing is already inside the published record.

These results suggest a distinction that matters throughout the rest of the report. AI may improve measured output quality on a defined task. It may also create common ideas, common errors and common forms of presentation. Neither result establishes the value of a longer-term research programme.

Data and Method

The report uses a frozen sample of 11 English-language accounting and finance journals: The Accounting Review, Journal of Accounting Research, Journal of Accounting and Economics, Contemporary Accounting Research, Review of Accounting Studies, Accounting, Organizations and Society, The Journal of Finance, Journal of Financial Economics, The Review of Financial Studies, Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis, and Review of Finance. The sample is purposeful and is not a global census.

Crossref [7] supplied article metadata. The queries used each journal ISSN, the journal-article type and a publication window from 2014 through 2026. Only issue years from 2015 through 2025 were retained. DOI duplicates were removed. A fixed, documented title rule excluded nonresearch records: editor and editorial matter, front and back matter, issue information, announcements and society business, prize notices, conference programmes, reviewer lists, placement advertisements, corrections, retractions, book reviews, discussion pieces and introductions. The final dataset contains 9,044 unique research article records from 11 journals. Two limits matter. First, Crossref typing and indexing are not identical to journal-specific definitions of a research paper, and some residual miscellany may remain. Second, the 2025 counts are the latest available at the access date: Journal of Accounting and Economics records show a large one-year fall that is more consistent with a deposit gap than with a publication decision. The 2025 endpoint is therefore provisional. At the 2024 endpoint the count is 887, a 23.5% increase on 2015.

Only two journals had public submission records that could be used under the evidence rules. The Journal of Finance publishes calendar-year totals, and The Review of Financial Studies publishes rolling May-to-May periods. Their definitions are not sufficiently similar to pool. Figure 1 therefore indexes each journal to its own first observed period and a companion table keeps the reported counts visible. The other nine journals publish only rolling, non-comparable metrics such as submission-to-first-decision timelines or current acceptance rates, so those values are not pooled here.

Citation attention was tested but not used as a comparative measure. For the 2020, 2021 and 2022 Crossref research articles, we matched DOIs to OpenAlex records [8] and summed citations in the three calendar years starting with the Crossref issue year. Of 2,655 cohort articles, 2,578 (97.1%) were matched by DOI; the unmatched records were mostly OpenAlex redirects to SSRN versions. Twenty-six matched articles (1.0%) had no recorded citation in the window. Journal shares ranged from 0.0% to 3.1%, with no more than five zero-citation articles in any journal, and the differences are not statistically distinguishable. The exercise is reported in the data appendix; it is not used to compare journals.

A citation cohort is the set of articles sharing a publication year. This measure does not count human readers. Automated access, citation lag, incomplete indexing and differences in citation cultures all affect the result. The evidence can show uneven visible attention. It cannot establish that human readers have disappeared.

AI guidance was collected from the latest usable official journal guidance or editorial-policy page for each journal. Nine pages were observed live on 22 October 2026. The Review of Financial Studies and Review of Finance use the latest usable archived pages (April 2025 and June 2024) because their live pages blocked automated access. Visible text was searched for generative AI, artificial intelligence, AI-assisted tools, large language models, LLM and ChatGPT. A page was coded as showing explicit AI guidance when an AI term appeared with a policy term such as disclosure, authorship, accountability, responsibility or prohibition. For Contemporary Accounting Research, the publisher page now directs authors to the CAAA author-information page, and the linked CAAA statement supplies the explicit terms. A missing mention means only that the guidance was not visible on the captured page. It does not prove that a linked publisher policy is absent.

Editorial priorities were recoded by hand from the substantive author-guidance and editorial-policy text of each journal's captured page, using a six-category codebook. A criterion counts only where the page states it as an author requirement or editorial expectation, with a quotable sentence. Navigation menus, JEL browse lists, copyright and cookie boilerplate, proof-stage notices and publisher-wide template paragraphs were excluded. This is a measure of stated criteria, not editorial behaviour.

Crossref update filters were also run for retractions, errata and corrections. The resulting record counts are not clean retraction rates because one event can appear in both the original record and a notice. No rate is inferred.

Terms used in this report have precise meanings. Crossref and OpenAlex are open bibliographic databases: Crossref records publication metadata such as DOIs and ISSNs, and OpenAlex adds citation links. A DOI is a persistent identifier for a publication, and an ISSN identifies a journal. Contribution is used in its ordinary scholarly sense: a defensible advance in knowledge or practice, not a count of outputs. An indexed record is one that a database has matched to a publication. A desk rejection is a rejection before external peer review. Identification concerns whether a relationship can be interpreted as causal rather than merely correlated. Replication means that another researcher can reproduce the analysis from the documented data and code, within legitimate access limits. Generative-AI guidance is an explicit statement about permitted use, disclosure or accountability.

Findings

Research production increased, but the submission story is uneven

The Journal of Finance stands at 86.9 in 2024 on its 2015 base. The Review of Financial Studies stands at 108.5 in 2026 on its 2023 base, after dipping in 2025.

Submission counts tell a limited story. The Journal of Finance recorded 1,227 submissions in 2015, 1,333 in 2020, 1,307 in 2023 and 1,066 in 2024. The Review of Financial Studies reported 1,534 in its 2022-2023 period, 1,544 in 2023-2024, 1,474 in 2024-2025 and 1,664 in 2025-2026. These movements are inconsistent with a simple claim that AI caused a permanent increase in finance submissions.

Crossref-indexed research article output across the selected journals rose from 718 in 2015 to 918 in 2025, an increase of 27.9%. The path was not steady, and three journals published fewer articles in 2025 than in 2015. That endpoint is provisional: at 2024, two journals were below their 2015 level and the count was 23.5% above 2015. This is evidence of modest growth in indexed output, not an AI-driven explosion.

Accounting and finance counts fluctuate without a continuous rise. The two field series sum to 718 in 2015 and 918 in 2025, a 27.9% increase; the 2025 endpoint is provisional.

The data do not reveal one uniform trend because the journals use different definitions and because most journals in the sample did not provide submission counts that met the evidence rules. The absence of a clear post-2022 surge at these outlets does not imply that AI has no effect. It means the effect cannot be inferred from a short, selective series. Later research should test preprint volume, desk-rejection behaviour and submissions to lower-ranked and specialist journals.

Citation counts are not readership

We also tested whether citation attention had become more uneven. It had not, in any way the data can defend. After cleaning the article cohort and matching citations to the Crossref issue year, the pooled share of articles with no citation in their first three calendar years was 1.0%, and journal-level shares ranged from 0.0% to 3.1%. The zero counts are small, the intervals overlap and the measure is sensitive to when an article first appeared online. An earlier version of this brief reported large journal differences; that result came from a cohort that still contained front matter, announcements and articles with inconsistent publication years, and it is withdrawn.

This limitation matters for the original question. A fall in human readership remains plausible but unverified. Citation counts record visible scholarly use, not reading, and a zero count can mean no attention, delayed attention or an indexing gap. AI makes the distinction more important because text can be produced without being read.

Editorial gatekeeping remains selective, and decisions still take weeks

Acceptance and rejection rates add useful context. The Journal of Finance accepted 6.4% of 1,226 editorial decisions in 2023 and desk-rejected 43% of new submissions. Its median turnaround was 43 days. The Review of Financial Studies reported an 8.1% acceptance rate and a 33-day median turnaround in its 2024-2025 period, and 7.27% with a 30-day median in its 2025-2026 period [5].

The Journal of Finance reported a median of 48 days in 2021, 52 days in 2022 and 43 days in 2023. Its 2023 desk-rejection rate was also 43%. The Review of Financial Studies median fell to 30 days in its latest period. These figures show that fast triage is possible. They do not show that careful review has become easier or faster.

The evidence here cannot measure reviewer supply directly. But the pattern is consistent with a different bottleneck: the supply of reviewers who can test a claim, editors who can compare contributions, and researchers who can detect errors that a model will also miss. AI can help editors screen submissions. It can also increase the number of plausible but underdeveloped submissions requiring screening.

AI rules are visible on most author pages, but scope varies

Across the latest usable official pages, 8 of 11 journals showed explicit AI guidance in the visible text. The Accounting Review requires disclosure and states that AI cannot serve as an author [9]. Review of Accounting Studies states that large language models do not meet authorship criteria and that use should be documented [11]. Journal of Accounting Research, Journal of Accounting and Economics, Contemporary Accounting Research, Accounting, Organizations and Society, The Journal of Finance and Journal of Financial Economics also displayed explicit AI rules on their latest pages. The Journal of Finance, for example, publishes an AFA AI policy that makes authors responsible for fact-checking outputs, bars AI tools from co-authorship and asks for model versions and prompts when AI is used to create data or analysis [14]. The Review of Financial Studies, Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis and Review of Finance did not show explicit AI guidance on the pages captured.

Eight journals show explicit AI guidance and three do not. The status reflects visible page text, not compliance or the complete policy environment of the publisher.

The variation should not be read as a journal ranking. Wiley, Elsevier, Springer Nature, Oxford University Press, Cambridge University Press and the American Accounting Association maintain different policy structures. A journal page may link to a publisher policy rather than reproduce it, and capture dates differ. The finding is narrower than it first appears: AI rules are now common, but most of them address writing, disclosure and authorship rather than data, code, model use or verification. A reader arriving at an author page will not always see the full policy environment, and three pages showed no explicit rule at all.

A publisher-level example makes the accountability issue explicit. Elsevier's journal policy permits AI support for manuscript preparation but states that AI tools must not substitute for human critical thinking, expertise and evaluation. Authors remain accountable and must verify sources, disclose substantive use and protect confidential material [10]. This makes clear where responsibility remains, but it does not show that the policy prevents error.

For accounting and finance, the policy question is broader than manuscript writing. AI may process earnings-call transcripts, propose empirical specifications, classify disclosures, generate code and produce recommendations. A journal rule that covers only grammar support would leave the research process largely unaddressed. Journals and universities need procedures for model choice, prompts, data retention, versioning and independent verification.

Corrections and retractions show why the evidence record needs maintenance

Crossref returned 20 retraction-update records, 21 erratum records and 28 correction records across the selected journals for 2015-2025. These are filtered record counts, not retraction rates. One event can appear more than once, and reporting practices differ.

The practical implication is not that the journals are unreliable. It is that a useful research record must include later correction, replication and data provenance. A publication is one point in a continuing evidence record, not its final state. When models can produce polished explanations of weak evidence, the ability to maintain and correct the record becomes more valuable.

Author guidance uses different ideas of valuable research

The recoded author-page sample states data transparency on 5 of 11 pages, integrity and accountability on 4, reproducibility on 3, contribution on 2 and research design on 1. No captured page states relevance to practitioners or policy. The counts are lower bounds: two journals' captured pages carried no substantive guidance, and three hold their policy in linked files.

Data transparency is stated on 5 of 11 pages, integrity and accountability on 4, reproducibility on 3, contribution on 2 and research design on 1. Decision relevance is stated on none.

The gap is the finding. Only data transparency is stated by close to half the sample, and no journal states relevance to practitioners or policy as an evaluation criterion. Contribution to the literature is named by two journals and research design by one. Author guidance therefore specifies very little about what makes research valuable; most of that judgement stays with editors and referees, which is where AI-assisted submission volume will press hardest.

Discussion

The scarcity shifts

A better response to cheaper writing and coding is not to increase output. It is to shift effort toward assets that remain costly, difficult to copy or necessary for trust.

1. Problem selection

The ability to identify a question that matters to theory or a real decision becomes more valuable when articles are easy to generate. Judgment about importance cannot be automated by asking a model whether a topic is interesting.

2. Data and context

Proprietary audit data, loan records, internal controls, hand-collected governance information, field access and regulatory knowledge create a defensible research position. Public, standardised data remain useful, but their analysis is less distinctive when the analysis is easy to reproduce.

3. Verification

The supply of plausible claims may rise faster than the supply of people who can test them. Research infrastructure should make it easy to inspect data lineage, code, assumptions, failed tests and alternative explanations.

4. Accountability

Someone must decide which evidence is sufficient and accept responsibility when the recommendation is wrong. That decision requires domain knowledge, ethics and institutional judgment, not only model capability.

Basic coding and model use are no longer unusual advantages. They remain necessary. Basic writing is similar. A researcher can know Python, use a model well and still write a clear answer to an unimportant question. The advantage lies in combining technical ability with problem judgement, evidence access and accountable interpretation.

Critical skills need to work together

The practical response is not a longer list of software tools. Technical ability should support research judgement rather than replace it.

Programming and prompt fluency are still valuable because a researcher who can inspect data, build tools, evaluate a model and read its code has more control over the evidence. Model literacy is broader than prompt writing. It includes understanding training data, retrieval, context limits, uncertainty, evaluation, failure modes and the difference between fluency and reliability.

Productivity should mean more defensible work within a given time, not simply more output. The useful question is whether a workflow reduces the time and cost required to reach a defensible answer, or whether it merely increases the number of unfinished outputs. Workflow optimisation matters when it improves verification and reuse. It creates risk when it accelerates production faster than evaluation.

Workflow optimisation is necessary, but not sufficient

A useful AI workflow changes where human attention is spent. The objective is fewer errors, shorter iteration cycles and more auditable decisions. A workflow designed mainly to raise output volume can increase editorial burden without increasing knowledge.

  1. Frame a consequential question. State the decision or theoretical problem, the audience and what evidence would change an answer.
  2. Specify the claim before generating text. Identify the estimand, comparison, expected direction and limits.
  3. Map existing evidence. Use structured retrieval, maintain stable identifiers and verify every citation against the source.
  4. Secure data and rights. Document the source, permission, coverage and transformations. Keep confidential material out of tools that cannot protect it.
  5. Design before prompting. Let AI propose alternatives, but require human approval of the identification strategy, measures and falsification tests.
  6. Generate code with tests. Use the model to write code and tests, then rerun from a clean environment and compare outputs with an independent implementation.
  7. Red-team the result. Ask another model or colleague for the strongest competing explanation, then test the criticism against data.
  8. Write the argument, not just the prose. Use AI to critique structure and clarity, while a named human owns every factual claim and normative judgement.
  9. Preserve provenance. Retain data dictionaries, prompts where relevant, model versions, outputs, failed tests and decision notes.
  10. Maintain the contribution. Correct errors, update data, document replication and track use.

This workflow treats the model as a research instrument and a critic, not as a substitute for the researcher. The phrase “human in the loop” is insufficient if the human merely approves polished output. Oversight has to include the authority, time and competence to stop a result that does not meet the evidence threshold.

Publication remains a threshold, not a sufficient measure

The evidence reviewed here is not sufficient to show that universities have abandoned publication counts or replaced them with a new metric. A likely near-term outcome is hybrid evaluation: publication remains an eligibility threshold, while appointments, promotion and funding use a broader evidence portfolio. Publication is likely to remain visible because it provides peer evaluation, a durable record and a common language for comparison. Its weakness is that a count cannot distinguish a carefully verified contribution from a technically compliant paper.

A credible next step is a research contribution portfolio. It would report contribution across several dimensions rather than hide them inside one undisclosed number.

The portfolio is a design proposal, not a validated evaluation system. It has costs. Collecting evidence is burdensome. Weights can be gamed. Qualitative evidence can be captured by powerful institutions. Researchers without proprietary data may be disadvantaged. A portfolio should therefore expose its components and permit contextual weighting rather than claim that one number is neutral.

A defensible implementation would separate three layers: contribution quality, process integrity and external use. Contribution quality concerns problem selection and evidence. Process integrity concerns data, code, disclosure and correction. External use concerns citations, policy or practice. The three layers answer different questions and should not be collapsed without explanation.

How to prepare

Individuals. Build one or two deep problem agendas rather than a large set of disconnected papers. Develop a defensible data or field position. Learn to specify causal designs, test code independently and use models against a written evidence standard. Keep a portfolio record that shows what changed in understanding or practice, not only where work was published.

Doctoral programmes. Teach research design, measurement, data engineering, model evaluation and research integrity as one connected sequence. Require students to replicate a published result, document a data pipeline and defend the assumptions of an AI-assisted analysis. Writing instruction should focus on argument, uncertainty and evidence, not only prose generation.

Journals. Publish clear rules for AI use across writing, data work, modelling and review. Require enough provenance for readers to understand the chain from data to claim. Invest in reviewer support and experiments with verification, registered reports and evidence audits. Publish the limitations of operational statistics when comparable data are unavailable.

Departments and universities. Stop using paper counts as the only evidence of capability. Use contribution portfolios with transparent dimensions and contextual weights. Fund long-lived data infrastructure and research software. Recognise replication, correction and data curation as research work. Audit any new metric for exclusion, gaming and administrative cost.

The preparation plan can begin before institutions agree on a new metric. Over 90 days, map the data and fields that provide a genuine research advantage, establish a source and code ledger, and run one AI-assisted workflow with independent verification. Over 12 months, aim for one contribution that can be defended, one reusable data or method asset, one output aimed at a decision maker, and one act of replication or stewardship. The objective is not to use more tools. It is to build a record that remains credible when tools are cheap.

Research Opportunities

The evidence suggests several research questions that accounting and finance can answer better than general commentary on AI.

  • AI disclosure and review outcomes. Do mandatory AI-use declarations change reviewer assessments, revision requests or rejection rates after controlling for topic, author and journal? Journals with staggered disclosure rules provide possible comparison settings.
  • Submission composition. If AI lowers production costs, does it change the distribution of topics, methods, author experience or institutional affiliation? The question requires anonymous submission-level data, not public totals.
  • Data advantages and research value. Which accounting and finance contributions become more concentrated when public-data analysis is cheap? A useful design would compare fields with different dependence on proprietary, field or hand-collected evidence.
  • Verification costs. How much reviewer time is needed to detect fabricated references, coding errors, inappropriate tests and unsupported causal language? The answer matters for journal design and academic labour.
  • Collective diversity. Do AI-assisted research workflows produce a narrower set of questions and methods? The Doshi and Hauser result provides a reason to test this in accounting and finance without treating it as settled evidence.
  • Metric experiments. Which contribution-portfolio elements predict later replication, decision use or scholarly trust? The question requires prospective evaluation rather than another retrospective index.
  • Institutional accountability. How should responsibility be assigned when an AI-assisted model, disclosure classification or investment recommendation causes harm? This is a legal, ethical and governance question with direct accounting relevance.

These projects require access that will itself become part of the research contribution. The most valuable studies may use confidential review records, firm data, regulator access or longitudinal fieldwork. Public aggregation is useful for describing the environment. It is less useful for identifying the mechanisms that determine which research survives scrutiny.
